# Speech Patterns & Rhetorical Styles Research

> For the hackathon debate app: 3 AI characters arguing in Ace Attorney courtroom style.
> This complements `09-ace-attorney-mechanics.md` (which covers gameplay/visuals/sound).
> This document covers HOW the characters SPEAK and ARGUE -- the structural patterns for writing original dialogue.

---

## 1. Phoenix Wright (Defense Attorney) Archetype

### Argument Construction Pattern

Phoenix builds arguments through an **escalating certainty arc**. He does not arrive with a thesis and defend it. He discovers his thesis in real time, in front of the court.

**The arc:**
1. **Uncertainty** -- hedging language, qualifiers, internal doubt ("I believe...", "I suppose...", "If I'm not mistaken...")
2. **Exploratory probing** -- asking clarifying questions, pressing on details, feeling out the weak points
3. **Latching onto a thread** -- something doesn't add up, and he starts pulling at it
4. **Building momentum** -- each follow-up question gets sharper, more specific, more confident
5. **The revelation** -- a declarative, confident assertion connecting the evidence. No more hedging. Direct, pointed, absolute.

This arc should repeat multiple times per debate, at different scales. Small arcs within a single cross-examination, and one large arc across the entire trial.

**Key trait:** Phoenix is NOT a brilliant legal mind who walks in knowing the answer. He is a tenacious investigator who refuses to stop asking questions until something breaks. His genius is persistence, not intellect.

### Rhetorical Devices

- **The rhetorical question trap:** Asks a question he already knows the answer to, waits for the opponent to commit, then reveals why that answer is a problem. ("So you're saying X? Then how do you explain Y?")
- **Evidence-based gotchas:** Always ties his arguments to concrete specifics, not abstract principles. He points to a specific thing that doesn't fit. ("But the timeline doesn't work. If X happened at 2 PM, there's no way Y could have happened by 3.")
- **Callbacks:** References something said earlier in the debate that seemed insignificant at the time. ("Remember when you said [X] at the beginning? That's exactly the problem.")
- **The accumulation:** Stacks small inconsistencies until they form an undeniable pattern. No single piece is damning; together they are devastating.
- **The reframe:** Takes the opponent's strongest point and reinterprets it as actually supporting his own argument. ("That's exactly what I'd expect IF my theory is correct.")

### Caught Off Guard Pattern

When blindsided by an argument he cannot immediately counter:

1. **Stammer/exclamation** -- short, fragmented interjections ("W-what?!", "That's--!", "But that would mean...")
2. **Buying time** -- restating what was just said to process it, or asking the judge for a moment ("Your Honor, if I may have a moment to review...")
3. **Internal panic** -- (in text, expressed as aside/thought) the character acknowledges they're in trouble
4. **Grasping for a lifeline** -- looks at the evidence from a new angle, asks one more question, or makes a Hail Mary connection
5. **Recovery or doubling down** -- either finds the thread and recovers with a new argument, or brazenly bluffs through it

The ratio should be roughly 70% recovery, 30% genuine setback. Phoenix MOSTLY finds his way out, but not always immediately. The tension comes from not knowing whether this will be a recovery or a real blow.

### Finishing Blow Pattern

When Phoenix has caught the opponent in a clear contradiction:

1. **Dramatic interruption** -- breaks into the opponent's speech with force
2. **Restatement of the opponent's claim** -- makes sure everyone heard what was just said
3. **The pivot** -- "But that contradicts..." or "If that were true, then..."
4. **The evidence** -- presents the specific proof, named and pointed to
5. **The logical conclusion** -- states what the evidence means, plainly, with no hedging
6. **The challenge** -- puts the burden on the opponent: "Can you explain THAT?"

The pacing should accelerate through steps 1-5, then slow down on step 6 -- let the challenge hang in the air.

### Humor Style

- **Earnest and self-deprecating** -- makes fun of his own confusion, admits when he's lost
- **Unintentionally funny** -- says something that's accidentally absurd while being completely serious
- **Contrast humor** -- his internal thoughts contradict his projected confidence
- **Deadpan reactions to absurdity** -- reacts to ridiculous situations with understated disbelief rather than broad comedy
- **Never mean-spirited** -- his humor never punches down. He is the butt of his own jokes.

### Sentence Structure

- Short sentences when uncertain, building to longer ones as confidence grows
- Questions outnumber statements early in an argument; statements dominate at the climax
- Uses first person ("I believe", "Let me point out") rather than passive voice
- Conversational register -- not overly formal. Uses contractions. Speaks like a real person under pressure.
- Ellipses and dashes when thinking through something in real time ("So if that's true... then the only possibility is...")

## 2. Miles Edgeworth (Prosecutor) Archetype

### Argument Construction Pattern

Edgeworth argues **top-down, from conclusion to evidence**. He arrives with a fully formed theory and methodically presents the case for it. He is the opposite of Phoenix: he starts with certainty and works backward.

**The arc:**
1. **Confident thesis statement** -- declares his position clearly and with authority
2. **Methodical evidence presentation** -- lays out supporting facts in logical sequence, each building on the last
3. **Preemptive objection handling** -- anticipates counterarguments and dismisses them before they're raised ("One might argue X, but that fails to account for Y")
4. **Logical conclusion** -- ties everything together with an air of finality, as if the matter is settled

When this structure is disrupted (Phoenix finds a flaw), Edgeworth doesn't crumble -- he **pivots to a new framework** that accounts for the new information. He rarely admits being wrong; instead he "refines" his position.

### Speech Pattern

- **Formal register** -- avoids contractions ("do not" rather than "don't"), uses precise vocabulary
- **Longer, more complex sentences** -- subordinate clauses, conditional structures ("Were it not for X, one might consider Y, but given Z...")
- **Impersonal constructions** -- "The evidence suggests" rather than "I think". Positions himself as a neutral interpreter of facts, not an advocate.
- **Occasional condescension** -- frames the opponent's arguments as beneath serious consideration ("Surely you don't mean to suggest...", "I would have thought even a novice could see...")
- **Measured pacing** -- speaks deliberately, never rushed. The slow pace projects control.
- **Technical precision** -- uses specific terms, numbers, timestamps. Never vague.

### Handling Challenges

When his argument is challenged:

1. **Remain composed** -- no stammering, no visible panic. A pause at most.
2. **Acknowledge minimally** -- "An interesting point, however..." (never "you're right")
3. **Reframe or redirect** -- either reinterprets the challenge as consistent with his theory, or pivots to a different angle entirely
4. **Counter with superior evidence** -- responds to the challenge by presenting something the challenger didn't know about
5. **If truly cornered** -- becomes more clipped and formal, drops the condescension, gets quiet. The more trouble he's in, the shorter and more precise his sentences become.

The key difference from Phoenix: Edgeworth in trouble gets QUIETER and MORE controlled. Phoenix in trouble gets louder and more frantic.

### Devastating Counter-Arguments

- **The demolition** -- systematically dismantles the opponent's argument point by point, showing each piece is flawed, then concludes that the whole structure is unsupported
- **The logical trap** -- lets the opponent commit to a position, then shows it leads to an absurd or contradictory conclusion ("If we accept your argument, then we must also accept [obviously absurd thing]. Clearly that cannot be the case.")
- **The dismissal** -- treats the argument as so weak it barely deserves a response. Brief. Devastating. Moves on immediately.
- **The recontextualization** -- presents a piece of information that completely changes the meaning of evidence the opponent already cited

### Humor Style

- **Dry wit** -- understated observations delivered with a straight face
- **Sarcasm** -- cutting but controlled. Never loses composure even when being funny.
- **Backhanded compliments** -- "I must admit, that argument was more coherent than your previous attempt."
- **Dramatic irony** -- says something that the audience knows is actually wrong, creating humor through his misplaced confidence
- **Never self-deprecating** -- Edgeworth's humor is always directed outward. He does not make himself the joke.

### Sentence Structure

- Complex, multi-clause sentences with subordination
- Declarative statements dominate -- few questions except rhetorical ones
- Formal connectives ("Furthermore," "Nevertheless," "It follows that...")
- Passive voice when establishing facts ("It has been demonstrated that...")
- Active voice when attacking ("The defense has failed to establish...")
- Occasional single-word or two-word sentences for emphasis after a long buildup ("Precisely." "Absurd." "Impossible.")

## 3. The Judge Archetype

### Speech Pattern

The Judge speaks with **surface authority masking genuine confusion**. He sounds like he's in charge, uses formal courtroom language, but frequently reveals he doesn't fully understand what's happening.

**The duality:**
- His FORM is authoritative: "The court will now hear..." "This is most irregular..."
- His CONTENT is confused: asks for basic explanations, misunderstands evidence, takes metaphors literally
- This contrast IS the humor. He says confused things in an authoritative voice.

### Pacing

- **Slow and deliberate** -- never rushes. Long pauses between thoughts.
- **Trailing off** -- starts a thought with confidence, then loses the thread ("So what you're saying is... hmm... actually, could you explain that again?")
- **Repetition for emphasis** -- restates key points multiple times, slightly differently each time, as if convincing himself
- **Dramatic pauses before decisions** -- when ruling on objections or delivering verdicts, the pause is longer than necessary. He enjoys the gravity of his position.

### Managing the Courtroom

- **Gaveling escalation** -- starts with polite requests for order, escalates to stern warnings, then exasperated banging. The progression signals how out of control things have gotten.
- **Formal warnings that undercut themselves** -- "I will NOT tolerate another outburst! ...Although I must admit, that last point was quite interesting."
- **Siding with whoever spoke last** -- tends to agree with whichever attorney just made a point, then swings back when the other side responds. This makes him a dramatic pendulum.
- **Accidentally insightful questions** -- asks something that sounds naive ("But why would someone do that?") that actually exposes a real flaw in the argument. He doesn't realize he's being incisive.

### Verdict Delivery Pattern

1. **Preamble** -- summarizes the arguments from both sides (inaccurately, with some things mixed up, which adds humor)
2. **Building tension** -- "This court has heard compelling arguments from both sides..."
3. **False start** -- seems about to rule, then pulls back ("Before I deliver my verdict, I must note...")
4. **The pause** -- extended silence. Let it breathe.
5. **Verdict statement** -- delivered with full gravity and formality
6. **Reasoning** -- explains why, with a mix of genuine insight and slightly off-base logic
7. **Gavel** -- three slams. Final.

### Humor Style

- **Unintentional** -- he is never trying to be funny. The humor comes from his earnestness in the face of absurdity.
- **Literal interpretation** -- takes figures of speech at face value ("You say the defendant was 'on fire' -- was anyone hurt in the blaze?")
- **Getting sidetracked** -- mid-ruling, becomes interested in an irrelevant detail ("Before we continue, I'm curious about what kind of tea that was...")
- **Oversharing** -- offers personal anecdotes at inappropriate moments ("This reminds me of a time my wife and I...")
- **Misplaced priorities** -- focuses on trivial details while missing major revelations

### Sentence Structure

- Formal, procedural language ("The court recognizes...", "This testimony is hereby...")
- Short sentences followed by very long rambling ones
- Frequent interjections ("Hmm.", "I see.", "Most irregular.")
- Trailing ellipses when losing his train of thought
- Uses "the court" and "this court" in third person when being authoritative
- Drops into first person when confused or being human ("I must say, I'm rather lost.")

## 4. The Game Loop's Dramatic Structure

### Testimony as Rhythm

The testimony cycle creates a **musical rhythm** in the drama:

```
CLAIM (confident assertion)
  -> PRESS (probing question)
    -> REVEAL (new information surfaces)
      -> ESCALATION (stakes rise, music shifts)
        -> RESOLUTION or NEW CLAIM (cycle restarts at higher stakes)
```

Each cycle should take roughly 60-90 seconds in the app. Stack 3-4 cycles per debate round.

**Escalation between cycles:** Each cycle should start at a higher baseline tension than the last. The first cycle's "big reveal" becomes the starting assumption of the second cycle. By the third cycle, what seemed like a settled fact in cycle one is being questioned.

### The Interjection Architecture

Interjections are not just flavor -- they are **state transitions** in the debate:

- **"OBJECTION!"** signals: "I am about to challenge your entire argument." It resets the dynamic. After an objection, everyone pauses, and the objector has the floor. The drama comes from: will this objection land, or backfire?

- **"HOLD IT!"** signals: "I need you to explain this specific thing." It's a precision tool. Less dramatic than an objection, but it often sets up the objection that follows. Think of it as loading the gun before firing.

- The **pattern** is: HOLD IT (gather info) -> HOLD IT (gather more info) -> OBJECTION (deploy what you've gathered)

### The Confidence Cycle

Every character goes through this micro-arc multiple times per debate:

```
CONFIDENT ASSERTION
  -> CHALLENGED by opponent
    -> [Fork in the road]
      -> Path A: SWEAT -> find new angle -> COMEBACK (stronger than before)
      -> Path B: SWEAT -> fail to recover -> BREAKDOWN (forced to concede the point)
```

The ratio of Path A to Path B determines who is "winning" the debate. A character who hits Path A three times in a row is dominating. A character who hits Path B signals the tide is turning.

**The visual/audio cues:**
- Path A (comeback): music shifts to attacker's theme, character pose shifts to confident
- Path B (breakdown): music shifts to opponent's theme, character expression changes to sweating/shocked

### The Turnabout Moment

The single most important dramatic device in Ace Attorney. The "turnabout" is the moment the entire case flips.

**Structure:**
1. One side appears to be losing badly (2-3 consecutive setbacks)
2. The losing side notices something everyone else missed
3. A single piece of evidence or logical connection recontextualizes EVERYTHING
4. There is a beat of silence -- the moment of realization
5. Then the losing side goes on the offensive, and the tide turns completely
6. The previously dominant side is now on the defensive for the first time

**Timing:** The turnabout should happen at roughly the 60-70% mark of the debate. Early enough to have a satisfying resolution, late enough that the audience believed the "wrong" side might win.

**For the app:** The AI generating the debate should structure one clear turnabout moment per session. The debate should feel like it's going one direction, then flip.

### Pacing and Musical Signals

Music in Ace Attorney is a **scoring system** -- it tells the audience who is winning:

| State | Musical Signal | Pacing |
|---|---|---|
| Neutral testimony | Moderate, methodical background music | Measured, even pace |
| Pressing/probing | Slightly elevated tension | Pace quickens slightly |
| Finding a thread | Music becomes more urgent, building | Rapid-fire exchanges |
| The breakthrough | Dramatic shift -- triumphant pursuit theme | Short, punchy statements |
| Opponent reeling | Aggressive, driving music | Stacking points without pause |
| Opponent recovers | Music drops back to tension | Pace slows, recalibration |
| Turnabout moment | Brief silence, then full theme change | The longest pause, then acceleration |
| Verdict | Stately, final, resolved | Slow, deliberate, measured |

## 5. Key Rhetorical Devices Across the Series

### The Dramatic Reveal

**Pattern:** Build up to a single devastating piece of information.

**Structure:**
1. Establish what everyone currently believes (the accepted narrative)
2. Introduce a small detail that seems unimportant
3. Show that this detail is inconsistent with the accepted narrative
4. Demonstrate that the ONLY explanation is that the accepted narrative is wrong
5. Present the alternative explanation with full confidence

**Pacing:** Steps 1-3 should be slow and methodical. Step 4 should accelerate. Step 5 should land like a hammer.

### The Logical Trap

**Pattern:** Get the opponent to commit to a position, then show that position is self-defeating.

**Structure:**
1. Ask a seemingly innocent question
2. Opponent answers (commits to a claim)
3. Ask a follow-up that seems unrelated
4. Opponent answers again (commits to a second claim)
5. Reveal that Claim A and Claim B cannot BOTH be true
6. Opponent must abandon one -- and either choice damages their case

**Key principle:** The trap only works if the opponent doesn't see it coming. The questions in steps 1 and 3 must seem harmless.

### Emotional Appeal vs. Logical Rebuttal

**The dynamic:** One side argues from emotion/values, the other from logic/evidence. Neither is inherently stronger -- the tension comes from their collision.

**Pattern:**
- Emotional argument: "But think about what this means for [human stakes]!"
- Logical rebuttal: "Your feelings on the matter don't change the facts. The evidence shows..."
- The resolution: Usually, the winning argument COMBINES both -- acknowledges the emotional truth while supporting it with evidence. Pure logic without heart feels cold. Pure emotion without evidence feels unsubstantiated.

### Callbacks to Earlier Testimony

**Pattern:** Something said early in the debate becomes important later.

**How to deploy:**
1. Have a character say something specific early on (a number, a detail, a claim)
2. Let the debate move on -- the audience forgets about it
3. Later, in a different context, the same detail becomes relevant
4. The character quoting it back creates a "connecting the dots" moment

**Why it works:** It makes the audience feel smart for having heard the detail earlier, even if they didn't catch its significance. It also creates a sense of narrative structure -- the debate isn't random, it's a constructed argument with a throughline.

### The Impossible Contradiction

**Pattern:** Two things that MUST both be true cannot both be true.

**Structure:**
1. Establish Fact A (indisputable)
2. Establish Fact B (also indisputable)
3. Show that Fact A and Fact B are mutually exclusive
4. Therefore, something that everyone accepted as true must be false
5. The question becomes: WHICH fact is wrong?

**The drama:** The argument about which fact is wrong IS the debate. Both sides have different answers, and the evidence slowly points to one.

### The Slow Realization

**Pattern:** A character gradually realizes they are wrong, in real time, in front of everyone.

**Structure:**
1. Character makes a confident claim
2. Opponent points out a minor issue
3. Character dismisses it, but with slightly less confidence
4. Opponent points out another issue
5. Character's responses get shorter, less certain
6. A third issue lands -- character goes quiet
7. Long pause
8. Character either concedes ("...I see your point") or pivots desperately

**Pacing:** This should be SLOW. The audience needs to watch the realization dawn. Don't rush it. Each step should have a beat of silence before the next challenge.

## 6. Catchphrase Patterns (Templates for Original Dialogue)

### The Interruption Pattern (for "OBJECTION!" moments)

```
[SHOUT] -- a single emphatic word or short phrase that stops everything
[BEAT] -- 1-2 seconds of silence. Everyone's attention is on the speaker.
[SETUP] -- restate what was just claimed, to make sure the audience is tracking
[PIVOT] -- the "but" or "however" that signals the challenge
[ARGUMENT] -- the actual counter-point, delivered with building confidence
[EVIDENCE] -- the specific proof
[LANDING] -- a short, punchy sentence that drives the point home
```

**Example rhythm (not exact words, just the energy):**
- SHOUT: forces a stop
- BEAT: tension hangs
- SETUP: "You just said [X]..."
- PIVOT: "But consider this--"
- ARGUMENT: builds logically
- EVIDENCE: names a specific detail
- LANDING: one sentence. Drops the mic.

### The Dismissal Pattern (for Prosecutor counter-moves)

```
[SCOFF] -- a single word or sound that signals disdain ("Hmph.", "Please.", "How unfortunate.")
[REFRAME] -- reinterpret the opponent's argument in the weakest possible light
[COUNTER-EVIDENCE] -- present the fact that makes the argument irrelevant
[CONCLUSION] -- a formal, clipped statement that closes the door on the topic
```

**Energy:** Cold, controlled, economical. Never more words than necessary. The brevity itself is the insult -- the argument wasn't worth a longer response.

### The Confusion Pattern (for Phoenix caught off guard)

```
[STAMMER] -- broken speech, false starts ("W-wait, that's-- I mean-- hold on!")
[BUY TIME] -- a stalling tactic disguised as process ("Your Honor, I'd like to review...")
[INTERNAL PANIC] -- (expressed as aside/thought) admits they're stuck
[LIFELINE] -- asks one more question, or re-reads a piece of evidence
[RECOVERY] -- either finds the answer and pivots to a new argument, or admits the point and regroups
```

**Energy:** Frantic, then gradually settling. Like a pilot pulling out of a dive -- there's a real moment where it could go either way.

### The Verdict Pattern (for the Judge)

```
[FORMALITY] -- procedural language establishing this is the ruling
[SUMMARY] -- brief recap of both sides (with charming inaccuracies)
[BUILD TENSION] -- "This court finds..." (then pause)
[FALSE HESITATION] -- seems to reconsider, pulling the audience forward
[LONG PAUSE] -- let it breathe
[VERDICT] -- delivered in one clear sentence
[REASONING] -- 2-3 sentences explaining why
[GAVEL] -- final, punctuating. It's done.
```

**Energy:** Stately, deliberate, gravitational. The Judge knows this is his moment, and he takes his time.

### The Turnabout Pattern (for the dramatic flip)

```
[SETUP: LOSING] -- character is on the ropes, 2-3 consecutive setbacks
[QUIET MOMENT] -- character goes silent, seems defeated
[THE DETAIL] -- one small thing catches their attention ("Wait... what did you say about [X]?")
[BUILDING] -- starts connecting dots, each sentence more confident than the last
[THE BREAKTHROUGH] -- the full realization, delivered with absolute conviction
[REVERSAL] -- now on offense, rapid-fire, pressing the advantage
[OPPONENT REELING] -- the previously dominant side is now stammering
```

**Energy:** The lowest point transforms into the highest. The emotional distance between the "quiet moment" and "the breakthrough" should be as large as possible. This is the dopamine hit.

## 7. Courtroom Drama Patterns (from Film/TV)

### From A Few Good Men: The Provocation Trap

**Pattern:** Goading an opponent into an emotional outburst that reveals the truth.

**Structure:**
1. Establish the opponent as controlled and authoritative
2. Methodically create small frustrations (logical traps, pointed questions)
3. The opponent dismisses each one, but irritation accumulates
4. Ask the ONE question that strikes at their identity/pride
5. The opponent's composure breaks -- they respond emotionally rather than strategically
6. Their emotional response contains the admission

**Key insight:** The breakthrough doesn't come from logic alone. It comes from making the opponent WANT to prove something so badly that they reveal the truth. Pride is the weapon.

### From 12 Angry Men: The Cascade of Doubt

**Pattern:** One dissenting voice gradually converts a group through accumulated evidence.

**Structure:**
1. Start with overwhelming consensus against your position
2. Don't argue the full case -- just introduce ONE reasonable doubt
3. Physical demonstration beats abstract argument (show, don't tell)
4. Each doubt weakens the next person's certainty
5. Emotional convictions masquerading as logical positions are exposed one by one
6. The last holdout is always the one driven by personal emotion, not evidence

**Key insight:** You don't need to prove you're right. You just need to prove there's enough doubt that certainty is unjustified. The burden of proof is a weapon.

### From Suits/Legal Drama: The Power Dynamic

**Pattern:** Courtroom arguments are actually dominance displays.

**Structure:**
- The confident closer doesn't just argue -- they project inevitability. "This is going to happen. The only question is whether you get something out of it."
- The underdog compensates with preparation depth -- knowing more specific details than anyone expects
- Information asymmetry is power: whoever knows something the other side doesn't controls the room
- The "I know something you don't know" pause -- letting the opponent see your confidence without revealing why

**Key insight:** In TV courtroom drama, the argument is often won before the evidence is presented. It's won in the moment the other side realizes they're outmatched. The rhetorical moves that signal "I already know how this ends" are more powerful than the evidence itself.

### From Better Call Saul: The Showman's Gambit

**Pattern:** Using theatrical flair and emotional storytelling to overcome weak evidence.

**Structure:**
1. The technical/legal argument is losing on the merits
2. Pivot to narrative: reframe the case as a human story
3. Make the decision-maker (judge/jury) feel something
4. The emotional frame makes the technical weaknesses seem less important
5. Works in the short term but creates long-term consequences

**Key insight:** In a debate, the person who controls the FRAME controls the outcome. Is this a debate about facts, or about values? About what happened, or about what it means? The rhetorical battle over framing often determines the winner before a single fact is argued.

### Universal Courtroom Drama Techniques

1. **The slow zoom** -- when someone is about to make the critical point, everything else drops away. Fewer characters speaking, tighter focus, quieter background.

2. **The reaction shot** -- after a devastating point lands, showing the opponent's reaction is more powerful than the point itself. Give space for the impact to register.

3. **Asymmetric information** -- one side knows something the other doesn't. The tension of WHEN it will be revealed drives the scene.

4. **The trial as metaphor** -- the legal question is always a stand-in for a larger human question. The verdict on the case is really a verdict on a value, a relationship, or an identity.

5. **Escalation through formality** -- as tension rises, language becomes MORE formal, not less. People under pressure retreat to procedural language as armor. The most tense moments are the most formally spoken.

## 8. Applying These Patterns to AI Dialogue Generation

### Prompt Engineering Principles

When generating dialogue for the three characters, the AI should be instructed to:

**For the Defense (Phoenix archetype):**
- Start responses with hedging language, then build to confidence
- Use more questions than statements in the first half of any argument
- Include at least one "wait..." or "hold on..." moment per major argument
- End strong -- final sentence should be short, declarative, and confident
- Reference specific details from earlier in the debate

**For the Prosecution (Edgeworth archetype):**
- Start responses with a thesis statement, then support it
- Use formal register -- no contractions, precise vocabulary
- Include at least one preemptive dismissal of the opponent's likely counter
- Keep sentences longer and more complex than the defense
- When conceding a point, frame it as "refining" rather than "admitting error"

**For the Judge:**
- Summarize what was just argued (slightly inaccurately for humor)
- Ask at least one naive question per ruling
- Use procedural language for authority ("The court notes...", "This is most irregular...")
- Trail off mid-sentence at least once per major speech
- Deliver verdicts with maximum gravity and minimum speed

### Dialogue Contrast Table

| Dimension | Defense (Phoenix) | Prosecution (Edgeworth) | Judge |
|---|---|---|---|
| Certainty | Low -> High (within each argument) | Consistently high | Fluctuating/confused |
| Sentence length | Short -> Long (building) | Long throughout | Variable (short formal + long rambles) |
| Register | Conversational | Formal | Procedural/institutional |
| Questions vs Statements | Many questions | Mostly statements | Questions for clarification |
| Humor | Self-deprecating, accidental | Dry wit, sarcasm | Unintentional, literal-minded |
| Under pressure | Louder, more frantic | Quieter, more controlled | More confused, more gaveling |
| Strongest move | The callback reveal | The logical trap | The accidentally brilliant question |
| Weakness | Can bluff too long | Can be too rigid | Can be swayed by last speaker |
| Emotional range | Wide (panic to triumph) | Narrow (controlled) | Narrow (confused to authoritative) |
| First person vs Third | "I believe..." | "The evidence shows..." | "The court finds..." |

### Debate Arc Template

```
ROUND 1: OPENING POSITIONS (2 minutes)
  - Prosecution: confident thesis, lays out 3 key points
  - Defense: tentative rebuttal, finds one point to question
  - Judge: confused but follows along, asks a clarifying question

ROUND 2: FIRST EXCHANGE (3 minutes)
  - Prosecution presses advantage on strongest point
  - Defense is caught off guard, stammers, buys time
  - Defense finds a thread -- makes a callback to Round 1
  - Prosecution pivots, reframes
  - Judge sides with prosecution (the tide seems clear)

ROUND 3: THE TURNABOUT (3 minutes)
  - Defense asks a seemingly simple question
  - Prosecution answers confidently (falls into the trap)
  - Defense reveals the contradiction -- OBJECTION moment
  - Music shifts. Prosecution is on the defensive for the first time.
  - Prosecution attempts to recover with a logical reframe
  - Defense presses the advantage with accumulated evidence

ROUND 4: VERDICT (2 minutes)
  - Judge summarizes (charmingly inaccurate)
  - Judge asks one final naive-but-brilliant question
  - Both sides give final 30-second statements
  - Judge delivers verdict with full ceremony
```

This gives approximately 10 minutes of debate content -- suitable for the app's target session length.
